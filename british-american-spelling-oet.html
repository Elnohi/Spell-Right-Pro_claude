British vs American Medical Spelling: Complete OET Guide

OET accepts both British and American spelling — but mixing them in the same letter costs you marks. This guide covers every medical spelling difference you need to know, with a consistency checklist and free practice tool.

The One Rule That Matters: Consistency

OET does not penalise you for choosing British or American spelling. It penalises you for mixing them.

This is the single most important point in this entire guide. The OET Writing sub-test accepts both British English (used in the UK, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland) and American English (used in the US, Canada, Philippines). Neither is considered more correct than the other.

What examiners do penalise is inconsistency within the same letter. If you write "colour" in paragraph one and "tumor" in paragraph two, the examiner sees a candidate who doesn't have control over their spelling conventions. This affects your Linguistic Features score directly.

The practical advice: pick the variant you're most comfortable with, and stick with it throughout. If you trained in the UK, Australia, or New Zealand, default to British. If you trained in the US, Canada, or the Philippines, default to American. Don't switch mid-letter.

From a doctor's experience

When I sat the OET, I defaulted to British spelling because I'd trained in a British-system healthcare environment. The challenge wasn't knowing which variant to use — it was catching the moments where American spelling crept in from years of reading American medical journals. The word "esophagus" nearly cost me marks because I'd written "oesophagus" everywhere else in the letter.

The 6 Spelling Pattern Differences

Don't memorise individual words — learn the patterns. Each pattern covers dozens of medical terms at once.

Pattern 1: -our vs -or

British English uses -our where American English uses -or. This is the most visible difference and the one examiners notice first.

British: colour, tumour, behaviour, favour, honour, labour, odour, vigour
American: color, tumor, behavior, favor, honor, labor, odor, vigor

In medical contexts, this frequently appears in "tumour/tumor", "colour/color" (as in skin colour), and "behaviour/behavior" (patient behaviour).

Pattern 2: -oe- / -ae- vs -e-

This is the pattern that catches most healthcare professionals, because it appears in high-frequency medical terms that you'll use multiple times in a single OET letter.

British: oesophagus, oestrogen, orthopaedic, paediatric, anaemia, haemorrhage, faeces, diarrhoea, dyspnoea, oedema
American: esophagus, estrogen, orthopedic, pediatric, anemia, hemorrhage, feces, diarrhea, dyspnea, edema

Notice: the British forms retain the Latin/Greek diphthongs (ae, oe), while American English simplifies them to a single "e". If you learn this one rule, you can correctly predict the spelling of dozens of medical terms in either variant.

Pattern 3: -ise vs -ize

British English generally uses -ise; American English uses -ize.

British: hospitalise, specialise, immobilise, stabilise, catheterise, cauterise
American: hospitalize, specialize, immobilize, stabilize, catheterize, cauterize

Pattern 4: -re vs -er

British: centre, litre, fibre, metre
American: center, liter, fiber, meter

In OET writing, this most commonly appears in "centre/center" (as in "health centre" or "rehabilitation centre").

Pattern 5: -lled / -lling vs -led / -ling

British English doubles the "l" before suffixes; American English does not.

British: cancelled, cancelling, counselled, counselling, labelled, modelling, travelled
American: canceled, canceling, counseled, counseling, labeled, modeling, traveled

This appears frequently in OET referral letters: "The patient was counselled on..." or "The appointment was cancelled due to..."

Pattern 6: -ence vs -ense

British: defence, licence (noun), offence
American: defense, license, offense

Less common in medical writing, but "defence mechanisms" does appear in psychiatric OET contexts.

The shortcut

If you learn just two patterns — the -our/-or pattern and the -oe-/-ae-/-e- pattern — you'll cover over 80% of the British/American medical spelling differences that appear in OET writing tasks.

The Trap Words: Same in Both Variants

These words are spelled the same in both British and American English. Candidates sometimes "correct" them unnecessarily, introducing errors that weren't there.

The following medical terms do not change between British and American spelling:

Accommodation — double c, double m in both variants. Don't second-guess this one.
Occurrence — double c, double r in both.
Necessary — one c, two s's in both.
Recommend — one c, two m's in both.
Separate — "a" in the middle, not "e", in both.
Receive — "ei" after "c" in both.
Immediately — double m in both.
Assessment — double s twice in both.
Prescription — same in both.
Diagnosis — same in both.

A common exam-day error: candidates who are nervous about consistency start "correcting" words that don't need correcting. If you're unsure whether a word differs between British and American English, leave it as you've written it. The 6 patterns above cover nearly every word that actually differs.

How Accents Affect Spelling in OET Listening

The OET Listening sub-test uses speakers with British, American, and Australian accents. Pronunciation differences can lead to spelling confusion.

In the Listening sub-test, you hear medical professionals speaking in different accents. This can affect spelling in unexpected ways:

The "r" difference

British and Australian speakers typically don't pronounce the "r" at the end of words like "centre" or "litre" — which can make it harder to hear whether the word ends in "-re" or "-er". American speakers pronounce the "r" clearly, which can make "center" sound more natural even if you're writing in British English.

The vowel shift

Australian pronunciation of "ae" diphthongs (as in "paediatric" or "anaemia") differs from both British and American pronunciation. If you're not used to the Australian accent, you might mishear the vowel and second-guess your spelling.

How to prepare

Practice listening to medical content in all three accents before the exam. Accent ≠ spelling convention

The accent of the speaker in the OET Listening sub-test does not determine which spelling convention you should use. You can listen to an Australian speaker say "oedema" and write "edema" if you're using American English — what matters is consistency in your own writing, not matching the speaker's accent.

Pre-Submission Consistency Checklist

Run through this checklist in the last 3 minutes of your OET Writing sub-test, before you submit.

1. Pick your variant. Before you start writing, decide: British or American. Write it at the top of your rough notes as a reminder.

2. Check -our / -or words. Scan your letter for "colour", "tumour", "behaviour", "favour". Are they all in the same variant?

3. Check -ae- / -oe- words. Look for "anaemia/anemia", "oedema/edema", "paediatric/pediatric", "haemorrhage/hemorrhage". These are the highest-risk words for accidental mixing.

4. Check -ise / -ize verbs. "Hospitalise" or "hospitalize"? "Specialise" or "specialize"? Make sure all verbs ending in this suffix are consistent.

5. Check doubled consonants. "Cancelled" or "canceled"? "Counselled" or "counseled"? Pick one pattern and apply it everywhere.

6. Don't "fix" words you're unsure about. If you can't remember whether a word differs between British and American English, leave it alone. Changing a correct spelling to an incorrect one is worse than leaving it as-is.
