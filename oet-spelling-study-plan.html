A 4-Week OET Spelling Study Plan

From baseline test to exam simulation: a structured routine you can fit into 20 to 30 minutes a day.

By SpellRightPro Founder · Updated November 2026

  4. Week 3: Spelling inside real letters

How the four-week plan works

Spelling is the easiest part of the OET writing sub-test to improve quickly, because it is a closed set of skills: a finite vocabulary, a predictable set of error types, and a result you can measure every day. This plan assumes about 20 to 30 minutes of practice per day for four weeks. If you have less time, stretch each week across two.

The plan works through three stages: find your weak words, fix them with deliberate repetition, then test yourself under exam conditions. You will need a spelling practice tool, a notebook or note app for an error log, and two or three of your own practice letters.

Week 1: Baseline and error log

Before you study anything, find out where you stand.

  • Take a 50-word test from the free OET practice tool and write down every word you miss in an error log, with the correct spelling beside it.
  • Write one practice letter under timed conditions. Underline every word you were unsure of while writing, even if it turned out to be correct.
  • Sort your errors into types: double letters (accommodate, occurrence), silent letters (diaphragm, haemorrhage), vowel confusion (separate, definite), and British versus American forms (oedema versus edema).
  • Pick one spelling convention, British or American, and stick to it. Mixing them inside one letter is a visible inconsistency. See our British vs American spelling guide.
Why the error log matters

Most candidates revise words they already know because it feels productive. An error log forces you to spend your time on the words you actually get wrong.

Week 2: Targeted word practice

Spend this week on your own error list plus the highest-frequency clinical vocabulary.

  • Daily: practise 15 to 20 new words aloud, typing each from memory after hearing it.
  • Every second day: re-test the words you missed on the previous two days. A word is only “learned” after you have spelled it correctly on three different days.
  • Use chunks: split long words into syllables and say them in an exaggerated way (dia-phragm, haem-or-rhage, phar-ma-cy). Spelling a word as it is not normally pronounced is a reliable memory hook.
  • Group by body system: cardiovascular words one day, respiratory the next, then gastrointestinal, neurological and so on. Related words reinforce each other.
  • Watch confusables: read our guide to commonly confused medical words and add any pair you mix up to your log.

Week 3: Spelling inside real letters

Now move from single words to words in sentences, because exam errors usually appear while you are thinking about content.

  • Write two practice letters this week. After each, check every medical word against a reference and add errors to your log.
  • Learn the abbreviations you should write out in full in formal letters. Our abbreviations guide lists the ones that most often appear in case notes.
  • Practise your opening, referral reason and closing sentences until the standard medical words inside them are automatic. The referral letter guide shows the usual structure.
  • Re-test your complete error log from weeks 1 and 2 once at the start of the week and once at the end.

Week 4: Exam simulation

In the final week, simulate the real experience.

  • Take one full 45-minute writing task under exam conditions, with no dictionary.
  • Spend the last five minutes of the time on a spelling pass: read your letter slowly from the last sentence to the first so that you notice individual words instead of the story.
  • Review your error log once more, concentrating on the words that you have missed more than once.
  • Stop adding new words two days before the test. Use the remaining time on rest and light revision of the error log only.
On exam day

If you cannot remember a spelling, rewrite the word two different ways on your draft paper and choose the one that looks right. Visual recognition often works when recall does not. If you are still unsure, choose a simpler synonym you can spell with confidence.

What spelling alone cannot do

Spelling accuracy is only one of several criteria that examiners score. A letter with perfect spelling but unclear structure, missing information or an inappropriate tone will not reach a high score. Use this plan alongside practice on content selection and organisation, not instead of it.

Progress is not always smooth. If your error log is not shrinking after two weeks, shorten your sessions and increase their frequency. Ten minutes twice a day usually outperforms one 30-minute session.
